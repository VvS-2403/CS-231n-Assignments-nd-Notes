# Chapter 11: EM Algorithms

In this set of notes, we discuss the EM (Expectation-Maximization) algorithm for density estimation.

## 11.1 EM for Mixture of Gaussians

Suppose that we are given a training set $\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}\}$ as usual. Since we are in the unsupervised learning setting, these points do not come with any labels.

We wish to model the data by specifying a joint distribution $p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}) = p(x^{(i)}|z^{(i)})p(z^{(i)})$. 
- $z^{(i)} \sim \text{Multinomial}(\phi)$ (where $\phi_j \ge 0$, $\sum_{j=1}^k \phi_j = 1$, and the parameter $\phi_j$ gives $p(z^{(i)} = j)$).
- $x^{(i)}|z^{(i)} = j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \Sigma_j)$.

Let $k$ denote the number of values that the $z^{(i)}$'s can take on. Thus, our model posits that each $x^{(i)}$ was generated by randomly choosing $z^{(i)}$ from $\{1, \dots, k\}$, and then $x^{(i)}$ was drawn from one of $k$ Gaussians depending on $z^{(i)}$. This is called the **mixture of Gaussians model**. Also, note that the $z^{(i)}$'s are latent random variables, meaning that they're hidden/unobserved. This is what makes our estimation problem difficult.

The parameters of our model are $\phi, \mu$ and $\Sigma$. To estimate them, we can write down the log-likelihood of our data:
$$ \ell(\phi, \mu, \Sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x^{(i)}; \phi, \mu, \Sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \sum_{z^{(i)}=1}^k p(x^{(i)}|z^{(i)}; \mu, \Sigma)p(z^{(i)}; \phi) $$
However, if we set to zero the derivatives of this formula with respect to the parameters and try to solve, we'll find that it is not possible to find the maximum likelihood estimates of the parameters in closed form.

If we knew what the $z^{(i)}$'s were, the maximum likelihood problem would have been easy:
$$ \ell(\phi, \mu, \Sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \log p(x^{(i)}|z^{(i)}; \mu, \Sigma) + \log p(z^{(i)}; \phi) \right) $$
Maximizing this with respect to $\phi, \mu$ and $\Sigma$ gives the parameters:
$$ \phi_j = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n 1\{z^{(i)} = j\} $$
$$ \mu_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n 1\{z^{(i)} = j\}x^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n 1\{z^{(i)} = j\}} $$
$$ \Sigma_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n 1\{z^{(i)} = j\}(x^{(i)} - \mu_j)(x^{(i)} - \mu_j)^T}{\sum_{i=1}^n 1\{z^{(i)} = j\}} $$
Indeed, we see that if the $z^{(i)}$'s were known, maximum likelihood estimation becomes nearly identical to Gaussian discriminant analysis, with the $z^{(i)}$'s playing the role of class labels.

However, since $z^{(i)}$'s are not known, we use the EM algorithm.

### The EM Algorithm
The EM algorithm is an iterative algorithm that has two main steps. In the **E-step**, it tries to "guess" the values of the $z^{(i)}$'s. In the **M-step**, it updates the parameters of our model based on our guesses.

**Repeat until convergence:** {
  **(E-step)** For each $i, j$, set
  $$ w_j^{(i)} := p(z^{(i)} = j | x^{(i)}; \phi, \mu, \Sigma) $$
  
  **(M-step)** Update the parameters:
  $$ \phi_j := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)} $$
  $$ \mu_j := \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)} x^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)}} $$
  $$ \Sigma_j := \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)} (x^{(i)} - \mu_j)(x^{(i)} - \mu_j)^T}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)}} $$
}

In the E-step, we calculate the posterior probability of our parameters the $z^{(i)}$'s using Bayes rule:
$$ p(z^{(i)} = j | x^{(i)}; \phi, \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{p(x^{(i)}|z^{(i)} = j; \mu, \Sigma)p(z^{(i)} = j; \phi)}{\sum_{l=1}^k p(x^{(i)}|z^{(i)} = l; \mu, \Sigma)p(z^{(i)} = l; \phi)} $$
The values $w_j^{(i)}$ calculated in the E-step represent our **"soft" guesses** for the values of $z^{(i)}$. The EM algorithm is reminiscent of K-means, but with "soft" cluster assignments instead of "hard" ones. It is susceptible to local optima, so reinitializing at several different initial parameters is recommended.

## 11.2 Jensen's Inequality

Let $f$ be a function whose domain is the set of real numbers. Recall that $f$ is a convex function if $f''(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. In the case of vector-valued inputs, this generalizes to the hessian $H$ being positive semi-definite ($H \ge 0$). If $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x$, then $f$ is strictly convex (or $H > 0$). 

**Theorem (Jensen's Inequality):**
Let $f$ be a convex function, and let $X$ be a random variable. Then:
$$ \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[X]) $$
Moreover, if $f$ is strictly convex, then $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = f(\mathbb{E}[X])$ holds true if and only if $X = \mathbb{E}[X]$ with probability 1 (i.e., if $X$ is a constant).

> **Diagram Description**
> Consider a plot illustrating a convex function $f$ (solid curve). 
> - $X$ is a random variable that has a $0.5$ chance of taking the value $a$ and a $0.5$ chance of taking the value $b$ (indicated on the x-axis).
> - The expected value $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is given by the midpoint between $a$ and $b$.
> - The values $f(a), f(b)$ and $f(\mathbb{E}[X])$ are indicated on the y-axis.
> - The expected value $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ is the midpoint on the y-axis between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. 
> - Visually, because $f$ is convex, the curve dips below the straight secant line connecting $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$, making it explicitly clear that $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[X])$.

*Remark:* If $f$ is [strictly] concave (i.e., $f''(x) \le 0$ or $H \le 0$), Jensen's inequality holds with the direction reversed: $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] \le f(\mathbb{E}[X])$.

## 11.3 General EM Algorithms

Suppose we have a training set $\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}\}$ and a latent variable model $p(x, z; \theta)$. The density for $x$ is marginalized over $z$:
$$ p(x; \theta) = \sum_z p(x, z; \theta) $$
We wish to maximize the log-likelihood:
$$ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x^{(i)}; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \sum_{z^{(i)}} p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta) $$

Explicitly maximizing $\ell(\theta)$ can result in difficult non-convex optimization problems. The EM algorithm gives an efficient method by repeatedly constructing a lower-bound on $\ell$ (E-step), and then optimizing that lower-bound (M-step).

Consider a single example $x$ and any distribution $Q$ over $z$ ($\sum_z Q(z) = 1, Q(z) \ge 0$):
$$ \log p(x; \theta) = \log \sum_z p(x, z; \theta) = \log \sum_z Q(z) \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} $$
Since $f(x) = \log x$ is a concave function, we can apply Jensen's inequality:
$$ f\left( \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q} \left[ \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} \right] \right) \ge \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q} \left[ f\left( \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} \right) \right] $$
Thus:
$$ \log \sum_z Q(z) \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} \ge \sum_z Q(z) \log \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} $$

To make the bound tight for a particular $\theta$, we need the term inside the expectation to be a constant. This is accomplished by choosing:
$$ Q(z) \propto p(x, z; \theta) \implies Q(z) = \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{p(x; \theta)} = p(z | x; \theta) $$
Setting $Q$ to the posterior distribution of $z$ given $x$ ensures the bound holds with equality.

### Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)
We define the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)** as:
$$ \text{ELBO}(x; Q, \theta) = \sum_z Q(z) \log \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{Q(z)} $$
So that for all $Q, \theta, x$, we have:
$$ \log p(x; \theta) \ge \text{ELBO}(x; Q, \theta) $$

### The General EM Algorithm
For a full training set, we introduce $n$ distributions $Q_1, \dots, Q_n$:
$$ \ell(\theta) \ge \sum_i \text{ELBO}(x^{(i)}; Q_i, \theta) = \sum_i \sum_{z^{(i)}} Q_i(z^{(i)}) \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i(z^{(i)})} $$

**Repeat until convergence:** {
  **(E-step)** For each $i$, set:
  $$ Q_i(z^{(i)}) := p(z^{(i)}|x^{(i)}; \theta) $$
  
  **(M-step)** Set:
  $$ \theta := \arg \max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n \text{ELBO}(x^{(i)}; Q_i, \theta) = \arg \max_\theta \sum_i \sum_{z^{(i)}} Q_i(z^{(i)}) \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i(z^{(i)})} $$
}

### Proof of Convergence
EM guarantees that $\ell(\theta^{(t)}) \le \ell(\theta^{(t+1)})$:
1. In the E-step, $Q_i^{(t)}(z^{(i)}) := p(z^{(i)} | x^{(i)}; \theta^{(t)})$ makes the bound tight:
   $$ \ell(\theta^{(t)}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{ELBO}(x^{(i)}; Q_i^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) $$
2. In the M-step, $\theta^{(t+1)}$ maximizes this ELBO:
   $$ \ell(\theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \text{ELBO}(x^{(i)}; Q_i^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \text{ELBO}(x^{(i)}; Q_i^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) = \ell(\theta^{(t)}) $$
Hence, EM causes the likelihood to converge monotonically. 

### 11.3.1 Other Interpretation of ELBO
ELBO can be rewritten using KL divergence:
$$ \text{ELBO}(x; Q, \theta) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q}[\log p(x, z; \theta)] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q}[\log Q(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q}[\log p(x|z; \theta)] - D_{KL}(Q \parallel p_z) $$
Another form:
$$ \text{ELBO}(x; Q, \theta) = \log p(x) - D_{KL}(Q \parallel p_{z|x}) $$
This explicitly shows that the maximum of ELBO with respect to $Q$ is attained when $Q = p_{z|x}$, as $D_{KL} \ge 0$ and is minimized to zero.

## 11.4 Mixture of Gaussians Revisited

Re-deriving the updates for the mixture of Gaussians using general EM:

**E-step:**
$$ w_j^{(i)} = Q_i(z^{(i)} = j) = P(z^{(i)} = j | x^{(i)}; \phi, \mu, \Sigma) $$

**M-step (Maximizing ELBO w.r.t parameters):**
We maximize:
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^k w_j^{(i)} \log \left( \frac{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma_j|^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(x^{(i)} - \mu_j)^T \Sigma_j^{-1} (x^{(i)} - \mu_j) \right) \cdot \phi_j}{w_j^{(i)}} \right) $$

**Updating $\mu_l$:**
Taking the derivative with respect to $\mu_l$:
$$ \nabla_{\mu_l} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^k w_j^{(i)} \log (\dots) = -\nabla_{\mu_l} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^k w_j^{(i)} \frac{1}{2}(x^{(i)} - \mu_j)^T \Sigma_j^{-1} (x^{(i)} - \mu_j) $$
$$ = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n w_l^{(i)} \nabla_{\mu_l} \left( 2\mu_l^T \Sigma_l^{-1} x^{(i)} - \mu_l^T \Sigma_l^{-1} \mu_l \right) $$
$$ = \sum_{i=1}^n w_l^{(i)} \left( \Sigma_l^{-1} x^{(i)} - \Sigma_l^{-1} \mu_l \right) $$
Setting this to zero and solving for $\mu_l$ yields:
$$ \mu_l := \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_l^{(i)} x^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_l^{(i)}} $$

**Updating $\phi_j$:**
We extract terms depending on $\phi_j$ and add a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint $\sum_j \phi_j = 1$:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^k w_j^{(i)} \log \phi_j + \beta \left( \sum_{j=1}^k \phi_j - 1 \right) $$
Taking the derivative with respect to $\phi_j$:
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi_j} \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{w_j^{(i)}}{\phi_j} + \beta $$
Setting to zero gives $\phi_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)}}{-\beta}$. Using the sum constraint yields $-\beta = n$. Thus:
$$ \phi_j := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n w_j^{(i)} $$

## 11.5 Variational Inference and Variational Auto-Encoder (Optional Reading)

Variational auto-encoders extend EM algorithms to complex models parameterized by neural networks. Let $\theta$ be the weights of a neural network $g(z; \theta)$ mapping $z \in \mathbb{R}^k$ to $\mathbb{R}^d$:
$$ z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{k \times k}) $$
$$ x | z \sim \mathcal{N}(g(z; \theta), \sigma^2 I_{d \times d}) $$

Since computing the exact posterior $p(z | x; \theta)$ is intractable, we restrict $Q$ to a family $\mathcal{Q}$ and optimize:
$$ \max_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \max_\theta \text{ELBO}(Q, \theta) $$

- **Mean Field Assumption**: For discrete variables, we assume $Q_i(z)$ factorizes into independent coordinates: $Q_i(z) = Q_i^{(1)}(z_1) \dots Q_i^{(k)}(z_k)$.
- **Continuous Variables**: We assume $Q_i$ is a Gaussian with its mean and variance predicted by neural networks $q$ and $v$ (often called the **encoder**, while $g$ is the **decoder**):
  $$ Q_i = \mathcal{N}(q(x^{(i)}; \phi), \text{diag}(v(x^{(i)}; \psi))^2) $$

### Reparameterization Trick
To optimize the ELBO using gradient ascent over $\phi, \psi, \theta$, gradients w.r.t $\theta$ are straightforward:
$$ \nabla_\theta \text{ELBO}(\phi, \psi, \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}_{z^{(i)} \sim Q_i} [\nabla_\theta \log p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)] $$

However, finding gradients over $\phi, \psi$ is difficult because the sampling distribution $Q_i$ depends on them. The **reparameterization trick** rewrites $z^{(i)} \sim Q_i$ as:
$$ z^{(i)} = q(x^{(i)}; \phi) + v(x^{(i)}; \psi) \odot \xi^{(i)} \quad \text{where } \xi^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{k \times k}) $$
(where $\odot$ denotes the entry-wise product). 

This allows us to move the derivative inside the expectation over the fixed distribution of $\xi^{(i)}$:
$$ \nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{z^{(i)} \sim Q_i} \left[ \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i(z^{(i)})} \right] = \mathbb{E}_{\xi^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)} \left[ \nabla_\phi \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, q(x^{(i)}; \phi) + v(x^{(i)}; \psi) \odot \xi^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i(q(x^{(i)}; \phi) + v(x^{(i)}; \psi) \odot \xi^{(i)})} \right] $$
By sampling $\xi^{(i)}$, we can estimate this gradient efficiently, allowing the model to be trained end-to-end via gradient ascent.
